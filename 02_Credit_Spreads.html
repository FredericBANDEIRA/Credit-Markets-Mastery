The Five Major Spread Metrics

From simple maturity matching to full zero-curve discounting and optionality

I-Spread Interest Rate Swap Rate (SOFR / €STR) ❌ No (Interpolated Swap) ❌ No European bond desks, corporate debt origination vs swap curve.
Z-Spread (Zero-Vol) Spot (Zero) Curve (Govt or Swap) ✅ Yes (Each cashflow discounted) ❌ No Standard institutional metric for option-free straight corporate bonds.
Asset Swap (ASW) Floating Rate Note (SOFR/Euribor) ✅ Yes (Via swap legs) ❌ No Relative value desks, funding packages, synthetic credit replication.

1. Why G-Spread Is Flawed

The G-spread simply takes $YTM_{\text{corp}} - YTM_{\text{treasury}}$. This creates severe distortion when:

  • The yield curve is steeply sloped: a 10-year corporate bond with an 8-year duration is compared against a 10-year Treasury, distorting the true spread.
  • Cash flow profiles differ: high-coupon bonds return capital much faster than par Treasuries.
2. The Rigor of the Z-Spread

The Zero-Volatility Spread adds a constant basis point parallel shift ($Z$) to every spot zero rate $z_t$ across the benchmark curve to equate present value to price:

$$P_{\text{dirty}} = \sum_{t=1}^N \frac{CF_t}{\left(1 + z_t + Z\right)^t}$$

Z-Spread vs Asset Swap (ASW) Spread Mechanics

Par-Par Asset Swaps, Proceeds Asset Swaps, and the Premium/Discount divergence

An Asset Swap (ASW) converts a fixed-rate corporate bond into a synthetic floating-rate note (FRN) paying $\text{SOFR} + ASW$. In an institutional Par-Par Asset Swap:

Leg 1: Bond Purchase

The investor buys the bond at market price $P$. If $P \neq 100$, an upfront exchange is made with the swap counterparty to bridge the gap between market value and par ($100 - P$).

Leg 2: The Swap Package

The investor passes the fixed bond coupon $C$ to the dealer and receives floating reference rate + constant spread: $\text{Floating} = \text{SOFR} + ASW_{\text{par}}$.

The Par-Par Asset Swap Formula & Relationship to Z-Spread
$$ASW_{\text{par}} \approx \frac{C - S_{\text{swap}} + (100 - P) / \text{Annuity}}{P / 100} \approx Z\text{-Spread} + \frac{100 - P}{PV01}$$
💡
The Par Divergence Rule (Crucial for Interviews):
  • Par Bond ($P = 100$): $ASW = Z\text{-Spread}$. The two metrics are identical!
  • Discount Bond ($P < 100$): $ASW > Z\text{-Spread}$. In a par-par swap, the investor pays only $P < 100$ but receives $100$ at maturity from the issuer. The swap dealer amortizes this upfront capital gain into a higher running spread.
  • Premium Bond ($P > 100$): $ASW < Z\text{-Spread}$. The investor paid $P > 100$ but receives only $100$ at maturity; the running spread is penalized to absorb the upfront loss.

Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) & Callable Debt

Stripping embedded interest rate options to reveal true credit and liquidity premia

Many corporate bonds and high-yield notes are callable: the issuer reserves the right to buy back the bond at a specified call price (e.g. 100 or 102) if market rates fall. The bondholder has effectively sold a call option on bond price to the issuer:

Price Relationship
$$P_{\text{callable}} = P_{\text{straight}} - \text{Call Option Value}$$

Because the option value is positive, the callable bond trades at a lower price and a higher nominal yield than an identical non-callable bond.

Spread Relationship
$$\text{Z-Spread} = \text{OAS} + \text{Option Cost (bps)}$$

OAS is strictly less than Z-Spread for callable bonds! The extra spread in Z-spread is not compensation for credit risk; it is the option premium paid to the investor for capping upside.

Spread Decomposition & The "Credit Spread Puzzle"

Why market spreads are 2x to 4x larger than empirical default losses

In financial theory, one might naively expect a corporate bond spread to equal the expected loss rate: $\text{Spread} \approx \text{PD} \times \text{LGD}$. However, empirical studies show that pure default risk accounts for only 25% to 40% of investment grade corporate bond spreads. The remaining 60% to 75% is composed of risk and liquidity premia:

1. Expected Default Loss
$$\text{EL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD}$$

The actuarial baseline loss expected from historical default rates.

2. Credit Risk Premium
$$\text{CRP} = \lambda_{\text{mkt}} \times \text{Vol}_{\text{credit}}$$

Compensation for systemic correlation: corporate defaults cluster in recessions when investor marginal utility is highest.

3. Liquidity & Tax Premium
$$\text{LP} = \text{Funding} + \text{BidAsk}$$

Cost of holding illiquid inventory, financing haircuts in repo, and state/local tax differentials vs Treasuries.

Cash-CDS Basis & Relative Value Trading

Arbitraging the spread gap between synthetic CDS and physical cash bonds

The Master Basis Definition
$$\text{Basis} = \text{CDS Spread} - \text{Cash Bond Spread (over Swap or Benchmark)}$$
1. Negative Basis Trade ($\text{Basis} < 0$)

Occurs when $\text{CDS} < \text{Cash Spread}$ (cash bond is cheap relative to CDS).

• Execution: Buy Cash Corporate Bond + Buy CDS Protection.
• Payoff: Lock in net positive carry: $\text{Bond Yield} - \text{Funding Cost} - \text{CDS Premium} > 0$.
• Default Protection: If entity defaults, CDS pays $1 - R$, fully offsetting the cash bond loss!
2. Positive Basis Trade ($\text{Basis} > 0$)

Occurs when $\text{CDS} > \text{Cash Spread}$ (CDS is expensive relative to cash).

• Execution: Short Cash Bond via Repo + Sell CDS Protection.
• Drivers: Synthetic short demand from macro hedgers who cannot borrow physical bonds, or cheapest-to-deliver option in CDS physical settlements.

Credit Spreads Interview Masterclass

8 high-frequency desk and research questions on spread metrics and RV trading

1. What is the fundamental difference between G-Spread, I-Spread, and Z-Spread?
Spread Metrics ▼

Concise Answer:

  • G-Spread: Compares the corporate bond's YTM against the YTM of an on-the-run benchmark government bond. It does not account for yield curve shape or maturity mismatch.
  • I-Spread: Compares the bond's YTM against an linearly interpolated swap rate (e.g. SOFR/Euribor) of matching maturity.
  • Z-Spread: The constant basis point spread added to every spot rate on the benchmark zero curve to discount each individual cash flow to the bond's market price. It is the only metric among the three that properly accounts for the term structure of interest rates.
2. Why does the Asset Swap (ASW) spread diverge from the Z-Spread when a bond trades away from par?
ASW vs Z ▼

Concise Answer: In a standard Par-Par Asset Swap, the investor pays or receives the difference between market price and par upfront: $(100 - P)$. The swap dealer amortizes this upfront cash flow into the running spread:

  • Discount Bond ($P < 100$): $ASW > Z\text{-Spread}$. The investor receives the bond at a discount, and the swap dealer converts that upfront discount into a higher ongoing floating coupon.
  • Premium Bond ($P > 100$): $ASW < Z\text{-Spread}$. The investor pays a premium upfront, which penalizes and reduces the running spread over SOFR.

Only at par ($P = 100$) does $ASW = Z\text{-Spread}$.

3. Walk me through Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS). Why is OAS strictly less than Z-Spread for a callable bond?
Optionality & OAS ▼

Concise Answer: When an investor buys a callable bond, they are effectively buying a straight bond and selling a call option to the issuer on the bond's price:

$$P_{\text{callable}} = P_{\text{straight}} - \text{Call Option Value}$$

Because the bond price is lower, its nominal yield and Z-Spread are higher. The Z-Spread contains two parts: $\text{Z-Spread} = \text{OAS} + \text{Option Cost (bps)}$.

The Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) strips out the cost of this embedded option using a binomial rate tree or Monte Carlo simulation, isolating the pure credit and liquidity risk. Therefore, for a callable bond, $\text{OAS} < \text{Z-Spread}$.

4. Explain the Negative Cash-CDS Basis trade. If it offers positive carry with hedged default, why does it persist?
Basis Trading ▼

Concise Answer: A negative basis ($\text{CDS Spread} < \text{Bond Credit Spread}$) allows a relative value trader to buy the cash bond and buy CDS protection, earning a net positive carry while fully covered against default.

Why it persists (Limits to Arbitrage):

  • Funding & Repo Haircuts: Banks and hedge funds must fund the cash bond via repo, requiring 5%–15% equity haircut and paying repo financing spreads.
  • Counterparty Risk: The CDS protection seller could default simultaneously during a systemic crisis.
  • Delivery & Restructuring Risk: The CDS contract may expire before the bond matures, or the credit event definition might not cover certain debt restructurings.
5. What is the "Credit Spread Puzzle" and how do credit desks explain it?
Empirical Credit ▼

Concise Answer: The "Credit Spread Puzzle" refers to the empirical finding that historical default losses ($PD \times LGD$) explain only 25% to 40% of investment grade corporate credit spreads. Spreads are significantly wider than default actuarial losses would dictate.

Desk Explanations:

  • Systemic Risk Premium: Corporate defaults cluster heavily during macroeconomic recessions when investors' marginal utility of wealth is highest.
  • Liquidity Premium: Corporate bonds trade over-the-counter with wide bid-ask spreads and repo financing frictions compared to ultra-liquid sovereign debt.
  • Tax Differentials: In markets like the US, Treasury interest is exempt from state and local taxes, whereas corporate bond interest is fully taxable.
6. A 5-year BBB bond has a Z-Spread of 220 bps and its 5-year CDS trades at 170 bps. What trade do you execute?
Desk Strategy ▼

Concise Answer: The Cash-CDS basis is $\text{Basis} = \text{CDS} - \text{Cash} = 170 - 220 = -50\text{ bps}$ (Negative Basis).

Execution Strategy:

  • Buy the physical 5-year BBB cash bond yielding $+220\text{ bps}$ over swap.
  • Buy 5-year CDS protection paying $-170\text{ bps}$.
  • Net Gross Carry: $+50\text{ bps}$ per annum. Assuming repo funding costs are $\le 20\text{ bps}$, this locks in a clean $+30\text{ bps}$ net unlevered carry with fully mitigated default risk.
7. Why do callable bonds exhibit negative convexity at low yields?
Convexity Dynamics ▼

Concise Answer: Normal option-free bonds exhibit positive convexity: as yields decline, their price increases at an accelerating rate ($\partial^2 P / \partial y^2 > 0$).

However, for a callable bond, as yields fall below the coupon rate, the probability that the issuer will call the bond approaches 100%. The bond price becomes compressed and capped near the call price (e.g. 102). Consequently, as yields fall, price appreciation slows down and eventually halts, turning the price-yield curve concave ($\partial^2 P / \partial y^2 < 0$, negative convexity).

8. How does a Par-Par Asset Swap differ from a Market Value (Proceeds) Asset Swap?
Asset Swap Structures ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Par-Par Asset Swap: The swap notional is set equal to the par value ($100$) of the bond. If the bond trades at $95$, the investor pays $95$ for the bond, but pays $100$ on the swap notional, requiring an upfront exchange of $5$ points from the dealer.
  • Market Value (Proceeds) Asset Swap: The swap notional is set equal to the actual market value ($P$) of the bond. No upfront cash exchange is needed between the investor and swap dealer. The investor pays the actual coupon received and receives floating reference rate plus a spread scaled to the invested market value.
