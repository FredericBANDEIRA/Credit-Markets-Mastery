The Probability Foundation: Intensity-Based Modeling

Hazard rate $\lambda(t)$, cumulative default $F(t)$, and survival probability $S(t)$

In modern quantitative credit pricing, default is modeled as the first arrival of an inhomogeneous Poisson jump process governed by an instantaneous intensity or hazard rate ($\lambda(t)$).

1. Hazard Rate $\lambda(t)$

The instantaneous conditional default probability rate at time $t$, given survival up to $t$:

$$\lambda(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbb{P}(t < \tau \le t + \Delta t \mid \tau > t)}{\Delta t}$$
2. Survival Function $S(t)$

The unconditional probability that the reference entity survives past horizon $t$:

$$S(t) = \exp\left(-\int_0^t \lambda(u)\,du\right)$$ $$\text{If } \lambda = \text{const} \implies S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$$
3. Cumulative Default $F(t)$

The probability of defaulting on or before time $t$, plus the marginal density $f(t)$:

$$F(t) = 1 - S(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$$ $$f(t) = -S'(t) = \lambda(t) e^{-\lambda t}$$
💡
Marginal vs Conditional Default Probabilities: Be careful not to confuse marginal default probability with conditional hazard rate. The marginal probability of defaulting during year 3 is $S(2) - S(3) = e^{-2\lambda} - e^{-3\lambda}$. The conditional probability of defaulting in year 3 given survival through year 2 is $1 - e^{-\lambda} \approx \lambda$.

Valuing the Premium (Fee) Leg

Quarterly coupon discounting, default accrual, and Risky PV01 (RPV01)

The protection buyer pays fixed coupon $C$ on quarterly payment dates $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N$ until contract maturity $T$ or default. Each payment occurs conditional on survival:

The Complete Premium Leg Valuation Formula
$$\text{PV}(\text{Premium Leg}) = C \times \text{RPV01} \times \text{Notional}$$ $$\text{RPV01} = \sum_{i=1}^N \Delta t_i \, D(0, t_i) \, S(t_i) + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \Delta t_i \, D(0, t_i) \left[ S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i) \right]}_{\text{Accrual on Default}}$$
1. Regular Scheduled Annuity

The sum of quarterly day-count fractions ($\Delta t_i = \text{ACT}/360 \approx 0.25$), discounted by the risk-free curve $D(0, t_i)$ and weighted by the survival probability $S(t_i)$.

2. Accrual on Default Term

If default occurs between $t_{i-1}$ and $t_i$, the buyer pays accrued coupon for the fraction of the quarter survived. Under standard ISDA pricing, default is assumed to occur on average at the midpoint ($\Delta t_i / 2$).

Valuing the Protection Leg & Fair Par Spread

Contingent cashflow integration and continuous-time derivation of $S_{\text{par}} = \lambda(1 - R)$

The protection seller pays $(1 - R)$ at the random default time $\tau$, provided $\tau \le T$. In discrete quarterly implementation:

Protection Leg Present Value
$$\text{PV}(\text{Protection Leg}) = (1 - R) \sum_{i=1}^N D(0, \bar{t}_i) \left[ S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i) \right] \times \text{Notional}$$
⚡

The Continuous-Time Mathematical Proof

Why $S_{\text{par}} = \lambda(1 - R)$ under flat curves

Assume constant interest rate $r$ and constant hazard rate $\lambda$. Then $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ and $D(0, t) = e^{-rt}$.

1. Protection Leg:

$$\text{PV}(\text{Prot}) = (1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-rt} \left( -d S(t) \right) = (1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-rt} \left( \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \right) dt = \lambda(1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt$$

2. Premium Leg:

$$\text{PV}(\text{Prem}) = S_{\text{par}} \int_0^T e^{-rt} S(t) dt = S_{\text{par}} \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt = S_{\text{par}} \times \text{RPV01}$$

3. No-Arbitrage Equality:

$$\text{PV}(\text{Prot}) = \text{PV}(\text{Prem}) \iff \lambda(1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt = S_{\text{par}} \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt$$ $$\implies \mathbf{S_{\text{par}} = \lambda(1 - R)} \quad \text{Q.E.D.}$$

Credit Trading Desks Risk Greeks & Sensitivities

CS01, IR DV01, Recovery01, Jump to Default (JTD), and Spread Convexity

1. CS01 (Spread01) & Convexity

CS01 measures the change in contract mark-to-market for a 1 basis point widening in credit spread:

$$\text{CS01} = \text{Notional} \times \text{RPV01} \times 0.0001$$

Positive Spread Convexity: As spreads widen, protection buyers gain at a rate that slows down (as RPV01 compresses), but as spreads tighten towards zero, losses are strictly bounded. For sellers, convexity is negative!

2. Jump to Default (JTD)

The instantaneous P&L if the reference entity files for bankruptcy overnight:

$$\text{JTD (Buyer)} = \text{Notional} \times (1 - R) - \text{Dirty Upfront}$$ $$\text{JTD (Seller)} = -\text{Notional} \times (1 - R) + \text{Dirty Upfront}$$

JTD cannot be hedged with small delta spreads; it requires jump protection via bonds or options.

3. IR DV01 (Interest Rate Sensitivity)

$$\text{IR DV01} = \frac{\partial \text{Upfront}}{\partial r} = (S_{\text{par}} - C) \times \frac{\partial \text{RPV01}}{\partial r}$$ Higher interest rates decrease RPV01 (discounting future coupons more heavily). If $S_{\text{par}} > C$, an increase in rates slightly decreases the upfront value.

4. Recovery01 (Rec01)

$$\text{Rec01} = \frac{\partial \text{Upfront}}{\partial R}$$ Measures sensitivity to changes in the assumed auction recovery rate. A 1% decrease in $R$ (e.g. 40% to 39%) increases the Protection Leg PV, requiring more upfront from the buyer.

CDS Pricing Interview Masterclass

8 high-frequency quantitative interview questions on hazard rates and valuation

1. Derive the fundamental rule of thumb $S \approx \lambda(1 - R)$ in continuous time.
Derivation ▼

Concise Answer: Under constant hazard rate $\lambda$ and risk-free rate $r$, survival is $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$.

The Protection Leg pays $(1 - R)$ upon default: $\text{PV}(\text{Prot}) = (1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-rt} \left(\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right) dt = \lambda(1 - R) \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt$.

The Premium Leg pays par spread $S$ conditional on survival: $\text{PV}(\text{Prem}) = S \int_0^T e^{-rt} e^{-\lambda t} dt = S \int_0^T e^{-(r + \lambda)t} dt$.

Equating $\text{PV}(\text{Prot}) = \text{PV}(\text{Prem})$ cancels the common integral $\text{RPV01}$, yielding exact equality: $\mathbf{S = \lambda(1 - R)}$.

2. What is Risky PV01 (RPV01) and why is it always smaller than risk-free PV01?
RPV01 Dynamics ▼

Concise Answer: Risky PV01 (also called Spread01) is the present value of a 1 basis point ($0.0001$) annuity paid quarterly until maturity or default.

It is strictly smaller than risk-free PV01 ($\text{RPV01} < \text{PV01}_{\text{rf}}$) because cashflows are discounted twice: first by the interest rate discount factor $D(0, t)$, and second by the survival probability $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t} \le 1$. In distressed names with large $\lambda$, distant cash flows vanish, compressing RPV01 dramatically.

3. Explain the accrual on default convention in the CDS premium leg.
Accrual Term ▼

Concise Answer: CDS premium payments occur quarterly. If the reference entity defaults between coupon dates, protection was provided during those intermediate days. Therefore, the buyer owes accrued coupon from the last IMM date to default date $\tau$.

In pricing models, assuming default occurs uniformly across the quarter, the expected accrual is modeled as: $\frac{C}{2} \sum \Delta t_i D(0, t_i) [S(t_{i-1}) - S(t_i)]$. Ignoring this term undervalues the premium leg by 1 to 3 basis points.

4. How do you calculate Jump to Default (JTD) for a protection seller?
Risk & JTD ▼

Concise Answer:

$$\text{JTD (Seller)} = -\text{Notional} \times (1 - R) + \text{Dirty Upfront}$$

If default strikes overnight, the protection seller must immediately pay $(1 - R) \times \text{Notional}$ at the auction. If the seller originally received an upfront payment, that upfront reduces the net loss. On a $10M contract with $R = 40\%$ and zero upfront, the seller loses $-\$6.0M$ overnight.

5. Why do CDS protection buyers enjoy positive spread convexity?
Convexity ▼

Concise Answer: For a protection buyer, as credit spreads widen ($\Delta S > 0$), MtM gains accumulate rapidly. Although RPV01 decreases as spreads widen, the contract value converges to the maximum possible payout $(1 - R)$.

Conversely, if spreads tighten towards zero ($\Delta S < 0$), the maximum possible loss is strictly capped at the running premium or upfront paid. This asymmetric payoff structure gives protection buyers positive convexity ($\partial^2 \text{NPV} / \partial S^2 > 0$).

6. If a 5-year CDS trades at 400 bps and recovery is 40%, what is the implied hazard rate?
Mental Math ▼

Concise Answer: Using the exact relationship $\lambda \approx S / (1 - R)$:

$$\lambda \approx \frac{400\text{ bps}}{1 - 0.40} = \frac{400\text{ bps}}{0.60} = 667\text{ bps} = 6.67\% \text{ per year}$$

Over 5 years, the approximate survival probability is $S(5) \approx e^{-0.0667 \times 5} = e^{-0.333} \approx 71.65\%$, implying a 5-year cumulative default probability of $\approx 28.35\%$.

7. How does an increase in the risk-free interest rate affect CDS upfront and par spreads?
Interest Rate Risk ▼

Concise Answer: An increase in risk-free rate $r$ increases discount factors $e^{-rt}$ across all maturities, which:

  • Decreases both the Protection Leg PV and the Premium Leg RPV01.
  • Because protection payouts typically occur sooner than the full 5-year annuity on average, the ratio $\text{PV}(\text{Prot}) / \text{RPV01}$ is only weakly sensitive to interest rates, leaving $S_{\text{par}}$ virtually unchanged.
  • However, for existing contracts trading away from coupon ($S_{\text{par}} \neq C$), the dollar upfront $(S_{\text{par}} - C) \times \text{RPV01}$ shrinks because RPV01 contracts.
8. Explain the difference between pricing a CDS with flat hazard rate vs bootstrapped piecewise hazard rates.
Curve Bootstrapping ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Flat Hazard Rate: Assumes default intensity $\lambda$ is constant across all tenors ($S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$). Useful for quick mental math and single-maturity contracts, but cannot price curve trades.
  • Piecewise Bootstrapped Hazard Rates: Discretizes intensity into constant pillars between standard market maturities: $\lambda_1$ for $[0, 1Y]$, $\lambda_2$ for $[1Y, 3Y]$, $\lambda_3$ for $[3Y, 5Y]$, etc. It calibrates sequentially so the model exactly reprices quoted market spreads at every pillar without arbitrage.
