Credit Risk Taxonomy & The Three Pillars

Default risk, downgrade risk, and counterparty exposure in Global Markets

In financial markets, credit risk is not simply the binary risk of a company filing for bankruptcy. It decomposes into three distinct trading and portfolio dimensions:

1. Default Risk

The catastrophic risk that the borrower fails to make scheduled interest or principal payments, triggering formal restructuring or bankruptcy liquidation.

2. Migration (Downgrade) Risk

The risk that an issuer's credit rating is cut (e.g., BBB to BB "fallen angel"), causing bond prices to fall and forcing mandates to liquidate holdings.

3. Counterparty Risk (CCR)

Bilateral risk in OTC derivatives (swaps, forwards) where the counterparty defaults while the contract has positive mark-to-market ($MtM > 0$) for the bank.

The Master Credit Risk Equation
$$\text{Expected Loss (EL)} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD} = \text{PD} \times (1 - R) \times \text{EAD}$$
PD (Probability of Default):

The likelihood that the counterparty defaults over a 1-year horizon. Measured via internal credit rating models, external ECAI scales, or implied from market CDS spreads.

LGD (Loss Given Default):

The percentage of exposure lost post-default: $\text{LGD} = 1 - R$. Senior unsecured bonds average $R \approx 40\%$ ($\text{LGD} = 60\%$), whereas 1st lien loans recover $70\text{--}80\%$.

EAD (Exposure at Default):

The dollar exposure when default strikes. For loans: drawn balance + credit conversion factor ($\text{CCF}$) on undrawn facilities. For derivatives: current $MtM$ plus PFE add-on.

KMV–Merton Structural Model (Default as an Option)

Unifying corporate debt valuation with Black-Scholes-Merton option theory

In 1974, Robert C. Merton revolutionized corporate credit by treating equity and debt as contingent claims on the firm's underlying assets ($V_t$):

1. Equity as a Call Option

Shareholders have limited liability. At debt maturity $T$, if asset value exceeds debt face value ($V_T > D$), equity holders pay $D$ and keep the remainder ($V_T - D$). If $V_T < D$, they default and walk away:

$$E_T = \max(V_T - D, 0) \implies E_0 = \text{BSM\_Call}(V_0, D, r, T, \sigma_V)$$
2. Debt as Risk-Free Bond Minus Put

Bondholders receive either full repayment $D$ or take over the company assets $V_T$. Debt is equivalent to a default-free bond minus an implicit put option written to shareholders:

$$D_0 = V_0 - E_0 = D e^{-rT} - \text{BSM\_Put}(V_0, D, r, T, \sigma_V)$$
Distance to Default (DD) & Default Probability
$$DD = \frac{\ln(V_0 / D) + \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_V^2\right)T}{\sigma_V \sqrt{T}}, \quad \text{PD} = \mathcal{N}(-DD)$$

Risky Duration vs Risk-Free Duration

Why credit risk dampens interest rate sensitivity and alters hedging ratios

In Fixed Income, Macaulay and Modified Duration measure the weighted average time until cash flows are received. In credit markets, future cash flows are uncertain: the bond may default before maturity. Consequently, each cash flow must be discounted by both the risk-free rate $r$ and the survival probability $S(t)$:

Corporate Risky Duration Formula
$$D^* = \frac{1}{P_{\text{risky}}} \sum_{i=1}^n t_i \cdot CF_i \cdot e^{-r t_i} \cdot S(t_i)$$
1. Cash Flow Weight Decay

Because $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ decreases monotonically with maturity, distant principal and coupon cash flows carry significantly less probabilistic weight in the duration sum.

2. The High-Yield Duration Collapse

As credit spreads widen (distress), the risky duration collapses dramatically. A 10-year CCC corporate bond behaves like a 2-year equity surrogate rather than a 10-year rate instrument.

Bank Balance Sheets, RWA & Basel AIRB

Pillar 1 minimum capital, CET1 buffers, and the Vasicek asymptotic credit risk model

The Bank Capital Stack (Loss Absorbency)

Under Basel III/IV, banks must hold Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital against their Risk-Weighted Assets ($\text{RWA}$):

Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) $\ge 4.5\%$ (plus buffers up to 9–12%): Common stock and retained earnings. Absorbs losses on a going-concern basis.
Additional Tier 1 (AT1 CoCos) $\ge 1.5\%$: Hybrid perpetual bonds that write down or convert to equity when CET1 falls below triggers (e.g. 5.125% or 7.0%).
Tier 2 Capital $\ge 2.0\%$: Subordinated debt and general loan loss reserves. Absorbs losses on a gone-concern basis in liquidation.
Basel Advanced IRB Formula for Capital Charge ($K$)

Under the Advanced Internal Ratings-Based approach, capital charge $K$ is derived from the single-factor Vasicek asymptotic copula:

$$K = \left[ \text{LGD} \cdot \mathcal{N}\left( \frac{\mathcal{N}^{-1}(\text{PD}) + \sqrt{\rho}\,\mathcal{N}^{-1}(0.999)}{\sqrt{1 - \rho}} \right) - \text{PD} \cdot \text{LGD} \right] \cdot \text{MatAdj}$$
$$\text{RWA} = 12.5 \times K \times \text{EAD}, \quad \text{Min Capital} = 8\% \times \text{RWA}$$

Credit Markets Interview Masterclass

8 high-frequency interview questions for S&T, Credit Research, and CFA Level 2

1. Why is corporate bond duration shorter than an equivalent Treasury bond's duration?
Duration & Rates ▼

Concise Answer: Corporate bond duration is shorter because of credit risk discounting. Future cash flows (coupons and par repayment at maturity) must be discounted not only by the risk-free rate $r$ but also by the survival probability $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$.

Because $S(t)$ decreases over time, the distant par payment at maturity carries significantly less probabilistic weight in the duration calculation than in a risk-free government bond. In high-yield or distressed bonds, this effect collapses modified duration, causing the bond to trade more like equity sensitive to credit spreads than a long-duration rates instrument.

2. Walk me through how the Merton (1974) model views equity and debt as options.
Structural Models ▼

Concise Answer: Under Merton's structural model, a firm's equity is a European call option on the underlying asset value of the firm ($V_T$) with a strike price equal to the face value of debt ($D$):

$$E_T = \max(V_T - D, 0)$$

If $V_T > D$, shareholders pay the debt and keep the excess equity value. If $V_T < D$, shareholders exercise limited liability, walk away with zero, and default. Conversely, debt is equivalent to a risk-free bond minus a put option: $D_0 = D e^{-rT} - \text{BSM\_Put}(V_0, D, r, T, \sigma_V)$.

3. What is the difference between physical ($\mathbb{P}$) and risk-neutral ($\mathbb{Q}$) default probability?
Pricing Theory ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Physical ($\mathbb{P}$) Default Probability: The real-world historical frequency of default measured by rating agencies (S&P/Moody's) from empirical bankruptcy data. Used for loan provisioning and economic capital.
  • Risk-Neutral ($\mathbb{Q}$) Default Probability: The default probability implied from market prices of CDS and corporate bond credit spreads, assuming risk neutrality.

Desk Nuance: Risk-neutral PD is almost always $2\times$ to $4\times$ higher than physical PD ($\text{PD}_{\mathbb{Q}} \gg \text{PD}_{\mathbb{P}}$) because market spreads include compensation for taking credit risk (credit risk premium), market illiquidity, and jump-to-default risk.

4. Explain the difference between Expected Loss (EL) and Unexpected Loss (UL). How does a bank manage each?
Risk Management ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Expected Loss (EL): The statistical mean credit loss ($EL = PD \times LGD \times EAD$). It is considered a regular operating expense and is managed through loan loss reserves / provisioning (IFRS 9 / CECL) and priced directly into loan margins.
  • Unexpected Loss (UL): The volatility and extreme tail of credit losses beyond EL (e.g. at the 99.9% confidence level). It cannot be covered by provisions; it must be backed by equity capital (Common Equity Tier 1).
5. A 5-year corporate bond trades at a spread of 180 bps and recovery is 40%. What is the annual implied default probability?
Mental Math ▼

Concise Answer: Use the fundamental CDS/Spread rule of thumb: $S \approx \lambda \times (1 - R)$.

$$\lambda \approx \frac{S}{1 - R} = \frac{180\text{ bps}}{1 - 0.40} = \frac{180\text{ bps}}{0.60} = 300\text{ bps} = 3.0\% \text{ per year}$$

The annual implied risk-neutral hazard rate is approximately 3.0%. Over 5 years, the approximate cumulative survival probability is $S(5) \approx e^{-0.03 \times 5} = e^{-0.15} \approx 86.1\%$, giving a 5-year cumulative default probability of $\approx 13.9\%$.

6. Why does increasing asset volatility ($\sigma_V$) in the Merton model transfer wealth from bondholders to equity holders?
Corporate Finance ▼

Concise Answer: Because equity is a call option on firm assets ($E = \text{Call}(V, D)$), its Vega is strictly positive ($\partial E / \partial \sigma_V > 0$). Equity holders participate in all upside gains when $V_T \gg D$, but their downside is capped at zero by limited liability.

Conversely, bondholders have sold an implicit put option to equity. When volatility increases, the probability of firm assets falling below debt face value $D$ increases, increasing the value of the short put. Because firm value $V = E + D$ is unchanged, the gain to equity comes directly out of the market value of debt.

7. What is the difference between Through-the-Cycle (TTC) and Point-in-Time (PIT) credit ratings?
Rating Methodologies ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Through-the-Cycle (TTC): External rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) evaluate an obligor's creditworthiness across an entire economic business cycle (5–10 years). Temporary cyclical recessions do not cause downgrades unless the fundamental solvency of the firm is impaired.
  • Point-in-Time (PIT): Evaluates default probability given the borrower's current condition and today's macro environment. Credit default swap spreads and bank internal IFRS 9 staging models are PIT.
8. What happens to bank capital requirements under the Basel AIRB formula when asset correlation ($\rho$) increases?
Basel Regulation ▼

Concise Answer: When asset correlation $\rho$ increases, the regulatory capital charge $K$ increases substantially.

In the Vasicek single-factor model, correlation $\rho$ measures the sensitivity of individual borrowers to the common systemic macro factor. When $\rho$ is high, defaults cluster together during a downturn (less diversification benefit). To protect against extreme joint defaults at the 99.9% VaR level, the bank must hold a significantly larger equity buffer.
