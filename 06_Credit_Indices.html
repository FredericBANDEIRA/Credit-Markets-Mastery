🌐

The Benchmark CDS Index Families

CDX (North America/EM) and iTraxx (Europe/Asia) structure & conventions

Standardized CDS indices allow institutional investors to take macro credit views or hedge diversified corporate portfolios in a single, deeply liquid transaction. The market is divided into two primary geographic families managed by S&P Global (IHS Markit):

North America & EM

CDX Family

  • CDX NA IG: 125 equally weighted North American investment-grade corporate entities. Standard coupon: 100 bps. Quoted in spread (bps).
  • CDX NA HY: 100 North American high-yield corporate issuers. Standard coupon: 500 bps. Quoted in price (points of par, e.g. 102.50).
  • CDX EM: Emerging market sovereign and corporate reference entities.
  • Weighting: Equal weight at inception ($1/125 = 0.8\%$ for IG; $1/100 = 1.0\%$ for HY).
Europe & Asia-Pacific

iTraxx Family

  • iTraxx Europe (Main): 125 equally weighted European investment-grade entities. Standard coupon: 100 bps. Quoted in spread (bps).
  • iTraxx Crossover: 75 European sub-investment grade (mostly BB/B rated) credits. Standard coupon: 500 bps. Quoted in spread (bps).
  • iTraxx Financials: Senior Financials (30 bank/insurer names) and Subordinated Financials (30 subordinated bank debt names).
  • iTraxx Asia ex-Japan & Japan: Regional liquid corporate benchmarks.

The Semiannual Roll & Default Lifecycle

March 20 & September 20 lifecycle, on-the-run liquidity, and default handling

1. Semiannual Index Roll

Every 6 months (March 20 & Sept 20), a new Series is issued (e.g. Series 41 $\to$ Series 42). Firms that were downgraded or lost trading liquidity are dropped; newly liquid firms are added.

2. On-the-Run (OTR) vs Off-the-Run

The newest Series is **On-the-Run (OTR)** and commands $>90\%$ of market trading volume. Previous series become **Off-the-Run (OFR)**, trading at wider bid-ask spreads with declining liquidity.

3. Constituent Default Settlement

If 1 of 125 names defaults: $$\text{Payout} = \frac{1}{125} \times (1 - R) \times \text{Notional}$$ The index notional permanently drops by $1/125 = 0.8\%$, continuing to trade on remaining 124 names!

Index Quoting Conventions: Spread vs Price

Mathematical link between quoted spreads, dollar prices, and upfront settlement

Index Contract Constituents Fixed Running Coupon Quoting Convention Upfront Cash Formula
CDX NA IG 125 IG Names 100 bps Spread (bps) $\text{Upfront} = (S_{\text{quoted}} - 100\text{ bps}) \times \text{RPV01} \times \text{Notional}$
CDX NA HY 100 HY Names 500 bps Price (e.g. 102.50) $\text{Points Upfront} = 100 - P \implies \text{Upfront} = (100 - P)\% \times \text{Notional}$
iTraxx Europe (Main) 125 IG Names 100 bps Spread (bps) $\text{Upfront} = (S_{\text{quoted}} - 100\text{ bps}) \times \text{RPV01} \times \text{Notional}$
iTraxx Crossover 75 Sub-IG Names 500 bps Spread (bps) $\text{Upfront} = (S_{\text{quoted}} - 500\text{ bps}) \times \text{RPV01} \times \text{Notional}$
CDX HY Price-to-Spread Relationship
$$P = 100 - \left(S_{\text{implied}} - 500\text{ bps}\right) \times \text{RPV01}_{\text{index}} \times 100$$

When HY spreads tighten below 500 bps, the index trades above par ($P > 100$). The protection buyer receives upfront points from the seller!

The Index Skew (Basis) & Relative Value Arbitrage

Market Index Spread vs Theoretical Single-Name Basket Spread

Because an index is simply an equally weighted basket of $N$ single-name CDS contracts, no-arbitrage pricing suggests that the market index spread ($S_{\text{index}}$) should equal the intrinsic theoretical spread of the basket ($S_{\text{intrinsic}}$).

Index Skew Definition & Theoretical Basket Formula
$$\text{Index Skew} = S_{\text{index}} - S_{\text{intrinsic}}$$ $$S_{\text{intrinsic}} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N S_i \quad \text{or rigorously: } \sum_{i=1}^N \text{PV}(\text{CDS}_i) = \text{PV}(\text{Index})$$
Positive Skew (Index Rich)

$S_{\text{index}} > S_{\text{intrinsic}}$

Driven by heavy macro hedging demand. During risk-off panics, asset managers buy index protection rapidly, bidding up the index relative to individual names.

Arbitrage Trade: Sell Index Protection, Buy Single-Name Basket.

Negative Skew (Index Cheap)

$S_{\text{index}} < S_{\text{intrinsic}}$

Driven by structured tranche hedging and balance sheet constraints. Banks selling synthetic CDO tranches sell index protection to hedge, depressing the index spread.

Arbitrage Trade: Buy Index Protection, Sell Single-Name Basket.

Default Correlation Dynamics: Why Equity & Senior Move in Opposite Directions

The Gaussian copula framework and the correlation sensitivity ($\text{Vega}_\rho$)

In portfolio credit, default correlation ($\rho$) dictates how defaults cluster in time. Crucially, correlation affects different tranches in opposite directions!

Idiosyncratic Regime

Low Correlation ($\rho \to 0$)

When defaults are independent, the Law of Large Numbers dictates that a predictable moderate number of defaults will almost certainly happen.

  • Equity Tranche [0-3%]: Virtually guaranteed to be wiped out! Equity spread is extremely high.
  • Senior Tranche [15-30%]: Extremely safe; portfolio loss variance is tiny, so losses cannot reach 15%. Senior spread is near zero.
Systemic Shock Regime

High Correlation ($\rho \to 1$)

Entities default together or survive together. The bimodal distribution has mass at 0 defaults or total collapse.

  • Equity Tranche [0-3%]: There is now a strong probability that NO company defaults! Equity risk drops $\implies$ Equity spread tightens!
  • Senior Tranche [15-30%]: High risk of catastrophic joint collapse $\implies$ Senior spread widens dramatically!

Credit Indices & Tranches Interview Masterclass

8 high-frequency desk interview questions on rolls, basis trades, and correlation

1. How is a constituent default settled in an index, and what happens to the remaining contract?
Settlement ▼

Concise Answer: When 1 constituent out of 125 names in an index (e.g. CDX IG) defaults:

  1. Auction Discovery: The ISDA Determinations Committee holds a credit event auction to determine recovery rate $R$.
  2. Cash Settlement: The protection seller pays the buyer $\frac{1}{125} \times (1 - R) \times \text{Notional}$.
  3. Index Factor Reduction: The index notional factor is reduced by $1/125 = 0.8\%$ (e.g. from $1.000$ to $0.992$).
  4. Ongoing Contract: The index continues to trade on the remaining 124 names with the reduced notional. Future coupon payments are calculated solely on the surviving $99.2\%$ notional.
2. What is index skew and how does a proprietary trading desk arbitrage it?
Skew Arbitrage ▼

Concise Answer: Index Skew is the difference between the quoted market index spread and the intrinsic theoretical spread of its 125 underlying single names: $\text{Skew} = S_{\text{index}} - S_{\text{intrinsic}}$.

  • If Index is Rich ($S_{\text{index}} > S_{\text{intrinsic}}$): Sell index protection (receive higher spread) and buy protection on all 125 single names in equal weight. When the basis converges, close out for profit.
  • If Index is Cheap ($S_{\text{index}} < S_{\text{intrinsic}}$): Buy index protection and sell protection on the 125 single names.
  • Desk Constraint: Skew arbitrage requires significant balance sheet capital to fund 125 single-name bilateral swaps and manages operational roll costs.
3. Why does increasing default correlation help the equity tranche seller but hurt the senior tranche seller?
Correlation Math ▼

Concise Answer: Correlation ($\rho$) changes the **variance** of portfolio default losses:

  • Equity Tranche [0-3%]: When correlation is low, losses cluster tightly around the mean; several defaults occur with certainty, wiping out the equity tranche. As correlation increases, the probability of **zero defaults** rises significantly. The equity tranche has a greater chance of escaping unscathed, so its risk drops and its spread tightens (benefiting the seller).
  • Senior Tranche [15-30%]: When correlation is low, diversification prevents cumulative losses from reaching the 15% attachment point. When correlation is high, the fat right tail allows massive joint defaults to wipe through senior layers. Senior spread widens sharply (hurting the seller).
4. Why do institutional investors roll their CDS index positions every March and September?
Roll Mechanics ▼

Concise Answer: Institutions roll from Off-the-Run (OFR) to On-the-Run (OTR) because:

  1. Liquidity & Bid-Ask: OTR accounts for $>90\%$ of trading volume with tightest bid-ask spreads (often $0.25\text{--}0.5\text{ bps}$ vs $1\text{--}2\text{ bps}$ for OFR).
  2. Credit Quality Refresh: Ineligible or distressed names are removed during the roll, maintaining the index's pure high-liquidity benchmark status.
  3. Mandate Compliance: Many UCITS, pension funds, and ETF prospectuses strictly mandate holding the on-the-run series.
5. Why is CDX High Yield quoted in price while CDX Investment Grade is quoted in spread?
Market Conventions ▼

Concise Answer: Historical cash market convention and spread volatility:

  • CDX HY: High-yield corporate bonds trade as **dollar price** (percentage of par, e.g. 102.50) due to large default probabilities and high upfront points. Quoting CDX HY in points upfront / price aligns directly with the cash HY bond trading desks.
  • CDX IG: Investment-grade cash corporate bonds trade as a **spread over Treasuries** (e.g. +85 bps). Hence, quoting CDX IG in basis points aligns directly with IG syndicate and secondary cash desks.
6. What is base correlation and why was it invented to replace compound correlation?
Copula Models ▼

Concise Answer:

  • Compound Correlation Failure: Solved for a single correlation $\rho$ per bespoke tranche $[K_L, K_U]$. It failed because for certain market quotes (mezzanine tranches during stress), either no solution existed or two solutions existed (a non-monotonic pricing function). Furthermore, the correlation smile could not price bespoke non-standard tranches consistently.
  • Base Correlation: Redefines all tranches as standard equity-first-loss layers $[0, K]$. Any bespoke tranche $[K_L, K_U]$ is priced as the difference between two base tranches: $[0, K_U] - [0, K_L]$. The base correlation curve is monotonic, uniquely invertible, and easily interpolated across attachment points.
7. How do you delta-hedge an equity tranche position [0-3%] with the underlying index?
Delta Hedging ▼

Concise Answer: The tranche delta is defined as $\Delta = \frac{\partial \text{PV}_{\text{tranche}}}{\partial \text{PV}_{\text{index}}}$.

For an equity tranche [0-3%], leverage is immense ($\Delta \approx 15\text{x}$ to $20\text{x}$). To delta-hedge a $\$10\text{M}$ long equity protection position:

  • Sell $\$10\text{M} \times 16.4 = \$164\text{M}$ of index protection!
  • This leaves the desk spread-neutral for small parallel moves. However, the desk is exposed to severe **gamma**, **correlation risk ($\text{Vega}_\rho$)**, and **Jump-to-Default (JTD)** if an idiosyncratic name blows up overnight.
8. Explain the negative basis phenomenon in synthetic CDO tranches during the 2008 financial crisis.
Crisis Mechanics ▼

Concise Answer: During 2008, cash ABS/corporate bond yields blew out significantly wider than synthetic CDS/CDO spreads (a huge negative basis: $\text{Cash Yield} - \text{Swap Rate} > \text{CDS Spread}$).

  • Funding & Balance Sheet Squeeze: Cash bonds required funded balance sheet cash, which dried up as interbank repo markets froze.
  • Synthetic Liquidity: CDS and synthetic CDO tranches were unfunded derivative contracts requiring only margin collateral. Investors dumped cash bonds at fire-sale prices while using synthetics to hedge, resulting in massive dislocations between cash and synthetic markets.
