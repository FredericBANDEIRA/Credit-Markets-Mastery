Anatomy of a Credit Default Swap

Bilateral OTC risk transfer: Premium Leg vs Protection Leg

A Credit Default Swap (CDS) is an over-the-counter bilateral derivative contract that transfers the credit exposure of a reference entity (corporate or sovereign issuer) from a protection buyer to a protection seller without transferring ownership of the underlying debt.

1. The Premium (Fee) Leg Protection Buyer

The buyer pays a recurring quarterly premium ($C$) on the contract notional until maturity $T$ or until a credit event occurs:

$$\text{Quarterly Premium} = \text{Notional} \times C \times \frac{\text{ACT}}{360}$$

Accrual on Default: If default occurs between coupon dates, the buyer must pay the accrued premium from the previous roll date up to the exact default date $\tau$.

2. The Protection (Contingent) Leg Protection Seller

The seller pays zero if no default occurs. If an ISDA-determined Credit Event is triggered, the seller pays the loss severity:

$$\text{Protection Payout} = \text{Notional} \times (1 - R)$$

Where $R$ is the final recovery rate determined via the standardized ISDA Credit Event Auction.

💡
Bank Relationship Management & Synthetic Hedging: When a bank extends a large bilateral corporate loan (e.g. $200M to an unrated corporate client), asking to sell or syndicate the loan can damage client relationships or trigger loan transfer restrictions. By buying CDS protection in the OTC market (e.g. $150M), the bank synthesizes a hedged position, obtains regulatory capital relief, and retains 100% client confidentiality without the borrower ever knowing.

The 2009 Big Bang & Small Bang Standardization

How ISDA transformed the global CDS market from bespoke OTC to standardized trading

Quoted Coupon Traded at Par Spread (variable, e.g. 143 bps). Standard Fixed Coupons: 100 bps (IG) or 500 bps (HY). Fungibility, netting across dealers, central clearing (ICE Clear Credit).

The Six ISDA Credit Events

Formal contractual triggers that trigger CDS protection leg execution

1. Bankruptcy

The obligor dissolves, files for Chapter 11/7 bankruptcy, becomes insolvent, or is unable to pay its debts as they fall due. Universal across all corporate CDS.

2. Failure to Pay

Failure to pay scheduled interest or principal exceeding the contractual payment requirement (typically $1M) after expiration of any contractual grace period.

3. Restructuring

Reduction or deferral of interest/principal, subordination, or debt-for-equity swap in a distressed context. Subject to regional trading conventions (XR, MR, MM).

4. Obligation Acceleration

One or more obligations (loans/bonds exceeding materiality threshold) become due and payable before normal maturity due to a technical covenant breach.

5. Repudiation / Moratorium

Primarily for Sovereign CDS: The government disaffirms, rejects, or declares a moratorium on its public debt payments.

6. Government Intervention (2014)

Added in the 2014 ISDA Definitions post-Euro crisis: Mandatory debt write-downs, bail-ins, or debt conversions forced by a governmental regulatory authority (e.g. Credit Suisse AT1 bail-in).

The ISDA Credit Event Auction Process

How global dealers discover the clearing recovery price $R$ without physical bond squeezes

Prior to 2005, all CDS contracts were physically settled: the protection buyer had to locate and deliver physical defaulted bonds to receive par ($100$). During major insolvencies (e.g. Delphi, Fannie Mae), the synthetic CDS notional was $10\times$ larger than the entire physical bond supply, triggering violent short squeezes.

Stage 1: Initial Market Midpoint (IMM)

Participating dealers submit two-way quotation bids and offers for deliverable obligations along with customer physical settlement requests. The average forms the IMM, establishing the net open interest (excess buy or sell imbalance).

Stage 2: Clearing Price Discovery

Limit orders are matched against the net open interest until the imbalance is filled. The marginal transaction price becomes the Final Auction Recovery Price ($R$). All cash CDS globally settle at $(100 - R)\%$.

CDS Mechanics Interview Masterclass

8 high-frequency questions on CDS contracts, Big Bang rules, and settlements

1. Explain the two legs of a CDS and what happens to accrued premium upon default.
Contract Anatomy ▼

Concise Answer: A CDS consists of two legs:

  • Premium Leg: The protection buyer pays a fixed quarterly coupon (e.g. 100 bps or 500 bps) until contract maturity or default.
  • Protection Leg: The protection seller pays $(1 - R) \times \text{Notional}$ if an ISDA credit event occurs.

Accrual on Default: If default strikes between coupon dates (e.g. 45 days into a 90-day quarter), the protection buyer must pay the accrued premium from the previous quarterly roll date up to the credit event date. This is netted against the protection payout.

2. Why did the 2009 Big Bang protocol introduce fixed coupons (100 bps / 500 bps) instead of trading at par spread?
Big Bang Standardization ▼

Concise Answer: Pre-2009, CDS contracts traded at bespoke par spreads (e.g. 142 bps, 147 bps). This created thousands of non-fungible contracts that could not be easily offset or novated between dealers, accumulating massive gross counterparty exposures.

The Big Bang protocol standardized coupons to 100 bps (IG) and 500 bps (HY) with upfront cash exchanges. This made all contracts for a reference entity completely fungible, enabling central clearinghouses (ICE Clear) to perform multilateral netting, compressing gross notional exposures by over 80%.

3. How do you calculate dirty upfront from clean upfront on a CDS trade?
Upfront Settlement ▼

Concise Answer:

$$\text{Dirty Upfront} = \text{Clean Upfront} + \text{Accrued Interest}$$

In market quoting, dealers quote the Clean Upfront (as a dollar amount or points upfront: $\text{Notional} \times (S_{\text{par}} - C) \times \text{RPV01}$).

However, on settlement date $T+3$, the buyer must reimburse the seller for the coupon accrued since the last quarterly IMM roll date: $\text{Accrued} = \text{Notional} \times C \times \frac{\text{Days}}{360}$. The actual wire transfer is the Dirty Upfront.

4. Walk me through how a commercial bank uses CDS for relationship management without alerting the borrower.
Desk Strategy ▼

Concise Answer: When a bank lends a large sum to an important corporate borrower, it may breach internal risk limits or require excessive regulatory capital. Selling or syndicating the loan would require borrower consent and damage commercial ties.

Instead, the bank buys single-name CDS protection in the OTC market. Because a CDS is a pure derivatives contract between the bank and an external dealer (e.g. hedge fund or pension fund), the borrower is never notified, the loan remains on the bank's books, and the bank frees up regulatory capital while preserving the client relationship.

5. What are the six ISDA credit events and why was Government Intervention added in 2014?
Credit Events ▼

Concise Answer: The six events are:

  1. Bankruptcy
  2. Failure to Pay
  3. Restructuring
  4. Obligation Acceleration
  5. Repudiation / Moratorium (Sovereigns)
  6. Government Intervention

Why Government Intervention was added: During the 2011–2013 European sovereign and banking debt crisis, regulators introduced mandatory bail-in regimes (BRRD). Regulators forced debt write-downs or conversions into equity without formal bankruptcy or contractual failure to pay. The 2014 definitions added Government Intervention to guarantee that regulatory bail-ins (such as the Credit Suisse AT1 write-down) trigger CDS protection.

6. Explain the difference between XR, MR, and MM restructuring conventions.
Restructuring Types ▼

Concise Answer:

  • XR (No Restructuring): Restructuring is completely excluded as a credit event. Standard in North America for corporate debt.
  • MR (Modified Restructuring): Restructuring is a credit event, but deliverable obligations cannot mature more than 30 months past the restructuring date.
  • MM (Modified-Modified Restructuring): Standard in Europe (iTraxx). Allows deliverable obligations to mature up to 60 months for restructured debt (or 30 months for other debt), offering broader coverage for European corporate workouts.
7. Why did the CDS market transition from physical settlement to ISDA credit event auctions?
Settlement Mechanics ▼

Concise Answer: Under physical settlement, the protection buyer had to deliver actual physical defaulted bonds to receive $100\%$ par. However, synthetic CDS volume frequently grew to $5\times\text{--}10\times$ the total outstanding face value of physical bonds.

When a default occurred, thousands of protection buyers rushed into the market to purchase physical bonds to make delivery, triggering an artificial short squeeze that pushed defaulted bond prices up from 20% to 70%! The ISDA Credit Event Auction establishes a single, transparent cash clearing price $R$, completely eliminating physical squeeze distortions.

8. A 5-year CDS with a 100 bps fixed coupon trades at a par spread of 260 bps and RPV01 is 4.4. Who pays the upfront and how much?
Mental Math ▼

Concise Answer: The market par spread ($260\text{ bps}$) is greater than the fixed coupon ($100\text{ bps}$).

Because the buyer will pay only 100 bps per year instead of the fair 260 bps, the Protection Buyer must pay upfront cash to the Seller:

$$\text{Points Upfront} \approx (S_{\text{par}} - C) \times \text{RPV01} = (0.0260 - 0.0100) \times 4.4 = 0.0160 \times 4.4 = 7.04\%$$

On a $\$10\text{M}$ contract, the Clean Upfront is $\mathbf{\$704{,}000}$ paid by the protection buyer to the seller.
